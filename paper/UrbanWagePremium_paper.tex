\section{Introduction}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Workers in urban areas earn a 16 percent wage premium over their non-urban counterparts.
This gap is often referred to as the urban wage premium, and is a well documented fact in the urban economics literature (see for example \citet{rosenthal2004evidence} for a review). 
The literature has closely explored the drivers of this gap, which include the role of sorting and selection of high productivity individuals into cities \citep{Combes_Duranton_Gobillon_2008}. 
For example \citet{Yankow_2006} documents that up to two-thirds of the urban wage premium is driven by differences in worker skills. 
The literature has also closely studied the role of differential returns to experience in urban areas as a source of the wage premium \citep{Baum-Snow_Pavan_2012}
Outside of human capital explanations, researchers have explored the role of agglomeration economies and firm survival to explain the remaining gap (\citet{Glaeser_Mare_2001}, \citet{wheaton2002urban}, \citet{combes2012productivity}, \citet{behrens2014productive}).\footnote{ Recently, work has also shown that urban industry-level concentration has led to a markdown of wages as firms exercise monopsony power \citet{azar2020labor}, thus, in the absence of concentration, the urban wage premium may be even larger than previously thought.} 
While the importance of sorting, selection, and firm survival are well documented in recent data, less is known about the urban wage premium throughout the 20th Century, and how the role of sorting and selection varied over time.  
\cite{Boustan_Bunten_Hearey_2013} provide descriptive evidence of the rising urban wage premium throughout the 19th and 20th Century, which peaked around 1990, however, they do not isolate selection and sorting effects in their exercise. 
In this paper, we explore how the urban wage premium has evolved over the second half of the 20th Century and into the 21st Century, while addressing unobservable sorting following recent econometric advances introduced by \citet{altonji2018estimating}. 

In our empirical exercise, we first document the raw urban wage premium over time. 
We then consider how much of the gap is explained by differences in observable individual characteristics. 
Finally, we seek to understand to what extent spatial sorting on unobservables, such as choice over unobserved amenities or unobserved productivity, drives the urban wage premium. 
To net out the role of spatial sorting, we follow \citet{altonji2018estimating} and control for MSA level averages of individual characteristics.
Intuitively, \citet{altonji2018estimating} highlight that group averages of observables tell us something about how individuals optimally select or sort into locations, if one is willing to assume that average observables are are correlated with group averages of unobservables.
Thus, controlling for group averages of observables controls for many unobservables that drive selection and sorting.

Adopting this approach allows us to document the role of sorting on the urban wage premium prior to the construction individual-level panel datasets. 
Prior litrature such as \citet{Baum-Snow_Pavan_2012}, \citet{Glaeser_Mare_2001}, and \citet{Yankow_2006}), have utilized individual-level panel datasets to account for sorting on unobservables, which is not possible in many historical datasets. 


Consistent with prior literature, we document a large urban wage premium in the raw data, ranging from 20-35 percent over the postwar era. 
The raw urban wage premium is at a peak in 1940, and declines rapidly between 1940 and 1950, and then declines more slowly throughout the period after 1950. 
We also see that the premium begins to rise again after 1980, which closely follows the skills gap discussed in the literature %CITATIONS.
Controlling for observable individual characteristics reduces the urban wage premium. 
We find that individual characteristics explain XX - YY of the urban wage premium, a much smaller share than  \citet{Yankow_2006} describes, albeit, we have a much more limited set of observables. 
When we add MSA-level averages of individual characteritics and MSA average characteristics, following \citet{altonji2018estimating}, we find that the urban wage premium is about 1/3 the size of the estimate that only controls for individual observables. 
Importantly, we see that after netting out the role of spatial sorting, the urban wage premium drops rapidly between 1940 and 1960, and then levels off to about 5 percent throughout the remainder of the 20th and 21st Century. 
Our estimate of 5 percent in the period 1960-2015 is very similar to the estimates reported in \citet{Baum-Snow_Pavan_2012}, \citet{Glaeser_Mare_2001}, and  \citet{Yankow_2006} that use individual fixed effects models. 
The estimates prior to 1979 are new to the literature. 

This raises an interesting question, what explains the decline in the urban wage premium in the postwar era, or alternatively, why did spatial sorting become increasingly important?
In the second hald of the empirical exercise, we explore whether urban-rural convergence explains the pattern that we documented above. 
We follow the approach of Barro and Sala-I-Martin (1990,1991) and estimate the rate at which MSAs and non-MSAs converge over the second half of the 20th Century. 
A large literature has explored the convergence of incomes in the United States over the 20th Century (Easterlin, 1960, Liebergott, 1966, Wright, 1987, McClean and Mitchener, 1999).  
However, to our knowledge, no work has explored the convergence of the urban wage premium over time. 
We estimate that rural-urban wage convergence occurs at a rate of XXX between 1940 and 2015. 
We also consider the rate of convergence between different periods (1940-1960, 1960-2015) and find that it is fastest between XXX and YYY. 
Importantly, we also explore what factors led to differential convergence rates over time. 
In particular, a large literature has explored the role of structural transformation in the United States (Caselli and Coleman, 2001), demographic changes stemming from the Great Migration (Boustan 2016, Collins and Wannamaker, 2017, Derenoncourt, 2022), and growing agglomeration forces (Krugman and Venables, 1995). 
We follow Ganang and Shoag (2017) and highlight how the urban wage premium differentially converges in locations with different initial conditions along dimensions of initial industrial structure, demographics, and economic integration via the highway network. 

We find that the rural-urban premium converged from below in locations with initially low levels of manufacturing and from above for places with initially high levels of manufacturing. 
This pattern is consistent with Caselli and Coleman's (2001) pattern of structural transformation. 
We also find that location with initially high shares of black population converged from below. 
This is consistent with the Great Migration and structural transformation as blacks left agriculture in the South to seek employment in northern industrial areas. 
Finally, we find that locations that had initial Market Access advantages converged from above, which is consistent with the South and West experiencing relatively larger transportation improvements with the construction of the Interstate Highway System in the 1960s. 

A remaining question is why the urban wage premium has not fully converged. 
We follow Ganaong and Shoag (2017) and examine the role that land use regulation has on the convergence of the urban wage premium. 
We find that places where land use regulation is relatively low have converged, while locations with high levels of land use regulation have not.
This finding highlights how inelastic housing supply can generate inefficiencies in the spatial distribution of employment.

\subsection{The Rise of Cities}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

As of 2019 over 82 percent of Americans live in urban areas, up from just under 40 percent in 1900. 
As Marshall (1920) highlighted, the formation of cities can reduce the cost of transporting ideas, goods, and people through colocation.
Yet, at the same time, increasing density can create congestion externalities, such as traffic congestion, or, as documented in early American history, a worse disease environment. 
As urban economist have noted, city size, wages, and rents will be determined by a spatial equilibrium whereby utility of the marginal individual will be indifferent between locations (Rosen, 1979, Roback, 1982). 
Through the lens of spatial equilibrium, the (nominal) urban wage premium exists either due to differences in rents, compensating differentials for disamenities, or imperfect mobility that prevents the real wage differential from being arbitraged.  


First, both biological and mechanical innovations in agriculture altered patterns of crop specialization and the optimal mix of labor and capital, freeing both land and labor for alternate uses (\citet{caselli2001us}, \citet{michaels2012urbanization}, \citet{olmstead2001reshaping}, \citet{griliches1963sources}, and \citet{jung2020long}). 
Second, negative health externalities due to crowding in cities has been largely mitiagated through public health efforts. 
Early in US history, cities were dirty, unhealthy places that required a wage premium to attract workers to face risks associated with infectious diseases such as cholera, typhoid, yellow fever, and smallpox (\citet{cutler2005role}, \citet{beach2016typhoid}, \citet{ferrie2008water}, \citet{alsan2019watersheds}, and \citet{anderson2020re}). 
As urban health improved, the need to attract workers with high wages subsided, however, as population density increased, so too did rents.
Finally, there have been drastic changes in transportation that have facilitated the growth of cities, which at the sime time, altered the opitmal scale of production (Chandler, 1959, 1993). 

Cities initially formed in places where it was convenient to trade and process agricultural goods due to natural advantages \citep{Boustan_Bunten_Hearey_2013}.
With improvements in technology, the scale and scope of cities has changed. 
For example, new cities sprang up near navigable rivers and canals to support the shipment and transshipment of goods. 
Later, the railroads bypassed many previous cities and led to new urban centers.\footnote{As \citet{bleakley2012portage} demonstrate, aggolmeration forces allow cities to exist after their technological obscelence.}  
Cities also formed near large mineral deposits where extraction and processing benefitted from economies of scale (Glaeser, Kerr, and Kerr, 2015).
In the post-WWII era, there have been significant improvements in car and truck transportation that have further expanded and reshaped cities. 
For example, \citet{rephann1994new} and \citet{duranton2012urban} highlight the role of the interstate highways in expanding growth rates and city-level employment in the United States. 
In the context of shipping transportation improvements, \citet{brooks2018local} and \citet{ducruet2019all} highlight how population has expanded as a result of containerization, typically in land abundant cities.

In our empirical exercise below, we will test whether improvements in market access stemming from internal highway improvements contributes to both wage growth and the urban wage premium.

\subsection{Wage and Individual Characteristic Data}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

To measure the average earnings per worker (wages),  we use the IPUMS Full Count Census for 1940, the 1 percent sample Census for 1950 and 1970, the 5 percent sample Census for 1960, 1980, 1990, and 2000, and the 2005, 2010, and 2015 American Consumer Surveys. 
Our sample consists of adult men living in the continental US between the ages of 25 and 65 who are employed for at least 26 weeks in the sample year. 
We apply IPUMS person weights in our summary statistics and all our regressions. 

We compute average weekly earnings (wages) by finding total income divided by the number of weeks worked in the given survey year. 
For individuals earning the top income code, we set their total income to the lower top income cutoff multiplied by $1.5$.\footnote{These cutoffs are reported in IPUMS INCWAGE documentation. In 2015 \$, the cutoffs are \$84,500 for 1940, \$99,2000 for 1950, \$199,500 for 1960, \$309,000 for 1970, and \$225,000 for 1980. Afterwards, IPUMS reports income as the median wage above that year's cutoff.} 
If the urban wage premium is primarily driven by people in the top income group, our estimates of the urban wage premium will be biased downwards by this choice. 
All incomes are then multiplied by the Consumer Price Index to be in 2015 dollars. 
In order to have a consistent measure of occupation, we use the `occ1950' variable provided by IPUMS which harmonizes occupation codes across all our survey years.
In Table \ref{table:summ} we report the number of individual observations in each cross section.

Table \ref{table:summ} also reports the average weekly urban and nonurban wage in each time period. 
Two striking trends appear from the summary statistics.
First, as we noted previously, the share of the population living in an urban areas has steadily increased between 1940 and 2015. 
This trend matches those highlighted in \citet{Boustan_Bunten_Hearey_2013}. 
Secondly, it is clear that both urban and nonurban mean weekly wages are growing over time in 2015 \$ from \$33.24 and \$25.23 in 1940 to \$1353.95 and \$1067.48 in 2015.
The urban wage premium, the ratio of the urban to nonurban wage, follows a W shape. 
The urban premium starts at 0.32, and then falls sharply between 1940 and 1950, where it declines to a low of 19 percent in 1980. 
Since 1980, the urban wage premium has increased; in 2015 the premium was 26 percent.

\section{The Urban Wage Premium: \emph{1940-2010}}

\subsection{Regression Framework}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Our goal in this paper is two-fold. First, we aim to describe the observed urban wage premium from a historical perspective. For this purpose, we estimate the urban wage premium from 1940 to 2010 by comparing average weekly wages for people who live in urban and non-urban areas.\footnote{Weekly wages are adjusted for inflation using the BLS' consumer price index in each survey year.} For each census survey year, we run the following basic regression:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:reg}
    \log(\text{Wage}_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Urban}_i + \varepsilon_i,
\end{equation}
where $\text{Urban}_i$ is an indicator if worker $i$ resides in an urban area (as classified by the Census Bureau). Therefore, our estimate of $\beta_1$ captures the percent difference in weekly wages earned by urban-residing workers realtive to non-urban-residing workers. These estimates will serve as a baseline description of the differences in wages that we \emph{observe} in our survey data and are not interpreted as the ``causal effect'' of living in an urban area.

One potential difficulty in comparing estimates across years is that the Census Bureau clasifies new (typically smaller) urban areas throughout the decades. There is evidence that the urban wage premium grows with city size and therefore we might expect to see a declining premium over time due to an expansion of urban area status.\footnote{For example, see \citet{Glaeser_Mare_2001}.} In a robustness exercise in the Online Appendix, Figure \ref{fig:premium_msa_in_all_yrs} reestimates our specification using only urban areas as classified in 1940. The results are very similar in this specification. 

The second goal of this paper is to understand the different sources underlying the wage premium. The unadjusted urban premium reflects a combination of a true causal wage premium; differences in skills due to endogenous selection of labor into urban areas; differential returns to labor market experience; and other potential city labor amenities such as agglomeration economies. To better understand how these different channels impact the urban wage premium, we extend our regression specification to control for confounding variables. Specifically, we estimate a set of regressions for each census survey year as follows: 

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:controls}
    \log(\text{Wage}_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Urban}_i + X_{i} \delta + \varepsilon_i,
\end{equation}
where $X_i$ is a vector of control variables for worker $i$. To facilitate consistent comparisons across years, we use the same set of individual-level covariates in each survey-year. This limits the number of variables we include to a set of 5-year age bins, indicator variables for education level, and an indicator for being in a racial minority. Therefore our set of control variables are not able to control for all potential sources of endougenous sorting. For example, \emph{unobserved} individual attributes like motivation and aptitude can lead to sorting but not be controlled for by the econometrician (\citet{Yankow_2006} and \citet{Combes_Duranton_Gobillon_2008}). Thus, we do not provide a causal interpretation to the estimate of $\beta_1$ in equation (\ref{eq:controls}) either. The aim of this exercise is to determine the amount of the overall observed wage premium that these observable covariates are able to explain and how that may change over time.

In section \ref{sec:group}, we aim to provide a causal estimate of the urban wage premium over time by controlling for \emph{all} sources of endogenous sorting into cities. Since we do not observe the same worker in multiple survey years, we cannot follow previous papers in including individual-specific fixed effects. Instead, we follow the methodology recently proposed by \citet{altonji2018estimating} which provides conditions under which controlling for group-averages of observed variables controls for \emph{all} the unobserved covariates that we don't have individual-level data for.

The results of estimating equation (\ref{eq:reg}) is displayed in Figure \ref{fig:premium_urban}. The urban wage premium starts around 35\% in 1940, decreases to 25\% between 1950 and 1970, and starting in 1980 the premium converges to where it stands today at around 20\%. Our results match the estimates found in Figure 3 of \citet{Boustan_Bunten_Hearey_2013} for estimates before 1980 while our estimates in later years are smaller than their estimates.\footnote{The reason for the difference in estimates comes from our use of a regression framework. The method in \citet{Boustan_Bunten_Hearey_2013} estimates the following
\[ 
    \log( \hat{\mathbb{E}}[\text{Wage} | \text{Urban} = 1] ) - \log( \hat{\mathbb{E}}[\text{Wage} | \text{Urban} = 0] )
\]
which differs from the regression estimate
\[ 
    \hat{\beta}_1 = \hat{\mathbb{E}}[ \log(\text{Wage}) | \text{Urban} = 1] - \hat{\mathbb{E}}[ \log(\text{Wage}) | \text{Urban} = 0].
\]. The latter is a more precise estimate for average percent difference in wages.} Our estimates are close to others in the literature. Our unadjusted difference for the year 1990 and 2000 is smaller in magnitude than the estimate of between 21 and 32\% from \citet{Glaeser_Mare_2001} and similar in magnitude to 1980 and 2000 as documented in \citet{Baum-Snow_Pavan_2012} and \citet{Boustan_Bunten_Hearey_2013}. Overall, our unadjused estimates at the urban wage premium provide evidence for the overall decline in the `importance' of cities for wages over time, but do not provide evidence of a `modern resurgance' of the wage premium that is suggested by previous descriptive and historical work.

We report the urban wage premium estimates after controlling for individual characteristics in Figure \ref{fig:premium_over_time} by the line with circle markers along with the original estimates marked with squares. Our estimates suggest that in 1940, the individual-level controls explain about 1/8 of the unadjusted urban wage premium, but the shape of the premium over time remains consistent. However, the explanatory power of individual controls steadily grows over time. In 2015, the individual-level controls explain about 1/3 of the urban premium highlighting the importance of sorting of individuals in explaining wage premiums in the modern era. Across all years in the sample, we find that individual-level observables explain between 8\% and 33\% of the urban wage premium, with the individual characteristics explaining a larger portion of the premium since 1980.

\subsection{Methodology}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Although we include a small set of individual-level control variables, there are many sources of unobserved covariates that can sort between urban and non-urban areas and lead to differences in average pay. We use the method proposed by \citet{altonji2018estimating} to control for these unobserved variables. The proposed method relies on two key relationships in how individuals sort. First, group-level averages of covariates in urban and non-urban areas reveal information about the differences in various amenities that cause people to sort between urban and non-urban areas.\footnote{This can even include the wage premium itself.} That is, the averages of covariates in urban and non-urban areas are correlated with average amenity quantities in urban and non-urban areas. For example, \citet{Diamond_Moretti_2021} find that higher-educated workers prefer a lot of amenities that are more abundant in cities like more food options.
Second, individuals sort based on their unobserved covariates because of the \emph{same} set of amenities. That is, the average of unobserved covariates are also correlated with the average amenitiy quantities. Continuing with our previous example, preferences for more food options might be correlated with unobservable qualities like curiosity that can lead to higher productivity in the form of innovation. Hence, controlling for the average of observable covariates (e.g. education) should therefore, at least partially, control for averages of unobservable covariates (e.g. curiosity). 

\citet{altonji2018estimating} show that under a set of conditions discussed below (and more formally in the authors' work), there exists a \emph{linear} mapping between group-level averages of \emph{observed} covariates and group-level averages of \emph{unobserved} covariates.\footnote{This is true even when there is no correlation between unobservable and observable variables at an individual-level.} Therefore including group-level averages linearly in Equation \ref{eq:controls} will absorb \emph{all of} the observed urban wage premium that is due to sorting on unobservable covariates.\footnote{The group-level averages also will absorb the effects of the amenities that drive sorting, so changes in the wage premium coefficient reflect removal of both sources.}

The result proposed by \citet{altonji2018estimating} relies on a few assumptions. First, it is assumed that individuals choose locations following a logistic choice model with a latent function that models the appeal of locations as a function of both the observed and unobserved covariates. This is standard in the urban economics literature. The second assumption, called the `rank condition' in their paper, pertains with the set of factors that drive sorting along the observable group averages. Essentially, the set of factors that drive sorting on \emph{observable} covariates must contain \emph{all} factors that drive sorting based on \emph{unobservable} covariates. The intuition for this is that group averages reveal information on the amenity factors that cause sorting based on observed covariates. If a different set of amenity factors drive sorting based on the unobservable characteristics, then information on these group averages do not inform us on the unobservable characteristics. 

The second part of the rank condition is that the number of group-average variables must be larger than the total number of factors driving sorting into urban areas. The intutition for this is that regression will first `invert' the observed group-level averages to amenities, but this inversion is only possible if the mapping is one-to-one. If there were more amenities than group-average variables, then there would not be a unique inverse and therefore the mapping between observable averages and unobservable averages would break. This second condition warrants more discussion as initially it may seem that there are many amenities that drive sorting into or out of urban areas. However, these amenities are often highly correlated with one another. More fomally, the rank condition requires the effective rank of the group-average variables be larger than the effective rank of amenity variables. \citet{Diamond_2016} provide evidence using a principal component analysis on a very detailed set of amenities that, at least in the 21st century, the effective rank of amenities is around 5 (with three components explaining most of the variation). Therefore, one of our identifying assumption on our set of group-average variables to have a large enough effective rank to satisfy the rank condition which is estimated around 3-5. If this were not the case, then we would only \emph{partially} absorb the effects of individual sorting.

The group-level averages we include are the share of individuals of different racial/ethnic groups, the share of individuals at different levels of educational attainments, the share of individuals who were veterans, the share of individuals of different marital status, and a measure of market access which summarizes a location ability to trade with other areas.

Our metro area boundaries are the 1950 Standard Metropolitan Area for 1940 and 1950, the Metrpolitan Statitistical Areas for their respective year in 1960-2010, and the Core-Based Statistical Area for 2015. 

This list is modified slightly to include MSAs that are recorded in the IPUMS survey data but are not included in that year's crosswalk. The counties that the MSA covers are identified by hand using the shape files from NHGIS for that year or if missing from the previous year. 

For a given year, a metro area's market access is the geometric average of the corresponding counties' market access. Then, for our measure of market access for the nonurban portion of a state is the geometric average of all counties that are not within any metro areas. We include robustness checks for weighting the average by county population.
