\section{Data}
% --------------------------------------------------

To estimate the relationship between the urban wage premium and the role that market access has on the premium throughout the post war era, we draw on several datasets. First, to measure market access, we construct a new harmmonized decadal dataset of the domestic US Highway Network that we describe in more detail below. To measure the average earnings per worker (wages),  we use the IPUMS Full Count Census for 1940, the 1 percent sample Census for 1950 and 1970, the 5 percent sample Census for 1960, 1980, 1990, and 2000, and the 2005, 2010, and 2015 American Consumer Surveys. Our sample consists of adult men living in the continental US between the ages of 25 and 65 who are employed for at least 26 weeks in the sample year. We apply IPUMS person weights in our summary statistics and all our regressions. 

We compute average weekly wages by finding total income divided by the number of weeks worked in the given survey year. For individuals earning the top income code, we set their total income to the lower top income cutoff multiplied by $1.5$.\footnote{These cutoffs are reported in IPUMS INCWAGE documentation. In 2015 \$, the cutoffs are \$84,500 for 1940, \$99,2000 for 1950, \$199,500 for 1960, \$309,000 for 1970, and \$225,000 for 1980. Afterwards, IPUMS reports income as the median wage above that year's cutoff.} If the urban wage premium is primarily driven by people in the top income group, our estimates of the urban wage premium will be biased downwards by this choice. All incomes are then multiplied by the Consumer Price Index to be in 2015 dollars. In order to have a consistent measure of occupation, we use the `occ1950' variable provided by IPUMS which harmonizes occupation codes across all our survey years.

\subsection{Market Access Data}
% --------------------------------------------------

To construct a measure of market access, we follow the inter-regional trade literature developed in in \citet{Eaton_Kortum_2002}, \citet{Redding_Venables_2004}, and \citet{Donaldson_Hornbeck_2016}.\footnote{The particular trade model used is described in \citet{Jaworski_Kitchens_2019}.} A county $c$'s market access, $\text{MA}_c$, is given by this system of non-linear equations 
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ma}
    \text{MA}_{c} = \rho \sum_d \tau_{cd}^{-\theta} \text{MA}_d^{-1} Y_d,
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ is the trade elasticity with respect to trade costs, $\rho$ is a constant from the theoretical model, and $Y_d$ is the income of county $d$ measured by GDP. 

Our measure of the iceberg trade costs to ship from county $c$ to county $d$ in year $t$, $\tau_{cd}$, measures the fuel costs to travel to a county $d$ as well as the wage paid to the truck driver for the drive time. The formula for $\tau_{cdt}$ is given by 
\begin{align} 
    \label{eq:trade_cost}
    \begin{split}
        \tau_{cdt} &= \text{distance in miles}_{cdt} \times \text{cost per mile}_t \\
        &\quad + \text{travel time in hours}_{cdt} \times \text{hourly wage of truck drivers}_t
    \end{split}
\end{align} 
    
To solve the system of non-linear equations derived from the model requires detailed trade cost, $\tau{cd}, from every origin to each destination. We approximate the origin-destination trade costs in each year by measuring travel costs through the contemporary highway network. Our measure of trade costs relies on measuring the distance and time traveled through the highway network, which is then monetized using hourly wages and fuel cost in each period, as highlighted above. To measure the time and distance post 2000 (2005 and 2015), we rely on the detailed shapefiles maintained by the federal government. For years prior to 2000 we georeference atlases produced by Rand McNally in each decade 1940-1990, as the federal government did not begin maintaining its database until 1994. To create a time consistent network, we begin with the 2000 version of the Federal Highway Administration's shapefiles. Then, for each decade, we overlay scans of each Rand McNally atlas and edit the federal shapefile. Specifically, we delete road segments that were constructed between the current map year and 2000, and re-code segments that have been reclassified over time (i.e., segments of the US Highway network that became interstates, or paved roads that were previously gravel or dirt). We perform this procedure iteratively for each decade back to 1940. One advantage of this approach is that it locates roads based on modern GPS technologies to ensure accurate placement throughout time. \footnote{One drawback is that this approach may ignore roads that were important prior to the modern stock of infrastructure.} We then follow the process outlined in \cite{Jaworski_Kitchens_2019} to measure the point to point distance travel time through the network each decade. That is, we represent each location by its geographic centroid, connected to the network by a set of access roads. We assign a travel speed to each segment in the network according to its classification (state, federal, interstate) and surface (dirt, gravel, paved), according to the parameters in \ref{table:road_param}. We then solve for the least cost route through the network for all pairwise combinations of origins and destinations.\footnote{More details on how the trade costs are calculated can be found in Section III of \citet{Jaworski_Kitchens_2019}.}

The market access variable summarizes a county c's access to the transportation network by taking an average of other counties' market size weighted by both the trade cost to ship to that market and by all other counties access to that market ($\text{MA}_{d}^{-1}$). Therefore a county $c$'s market access is large when it has access to large markets that other counties have little access to.

To aggregate market access to Urban areas, we created a crosswalk of counties within MSAs. For each MSA, we average across the counties within the MSA.\footnote{Our results are robust to weighting by population. (NEED TO CHECK)} Our measure of non-urban market access for a given state is the average of counties in that state that do not fall within any MSA. Therefore, for each individual we have a measure of whether or not they live in an urban area and the market access of the urban/non-urban area they live in. This process is detailed in Appendix \ref{appendix:msa_ma}.

\section{Results}
% --------------------------------------------------

For an initial understanding of the urban wage premium, for each survey year we take the ratio between the average urban weekly wage and the nonurban weekly wage. This ratio replicates the method used in \citet{Boustan_Bunten_Hearey_2013} to calculate the urban wage premium. In Figure \ref{fig:premium_over_time}, the grey line with square markers shows the results of this exercise. The U-shape in which the urban wage premium starts around 30\% in 1940, decreases between 1950 and 1980 to just over 20\% and then increases again to over 25\% after 2000 is consistent with results found in the literature.\footnote{Our resuts match the shape over time found in Figure 3 of \citet{Boustan_Bunten_Hearey_2013}. Our estimate in 2000 is a few percentage points lower than the estimate of 32\% from \citet{Glaeser_Mare_2001}. The increase between 1980 and 2000 matches \citet{Baum-Snow_Pavan_2012}.}

Moving into a regression framework, we run versions of the following specification seperately for each survey year: 
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:reg}
    \log(\text{Weekly Wage}_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Urban}_i + \beta_2 \log(\text{Market Access}_{MSA(i)}) + \delta X_{i} + \varepsilon_i,
\end{equation}
where $X_i$ is a set of controls consisting of age in 5-year bins, education levels, and an indicator for being White. Our estimate of $\beta_1$ is the percent increase in wages that workers in urban areas have on average which we call the urban wage premium. The first regression only includes an indicator for living in an urban area without the log of market access or controls $X_i$. This is the regression based version of the \citet{Boustan_Bunten_Hearey_2013} approach. However, there can be demographic differences between urban and rural areas that contribute to wage differentials and so we add controls for education, race, and age in the following specifications.

The wages of individuals in Urban areas also benefit from the improved transportation network, so in the final specification, we add in the log of market access. After controlling for market access, the coefficient on Urban, $\beta_1$ should pick up on the non-transportation causes of the urban wage premium.\footnote{This includes the improved human capital development from \citet{Glaeser_Mare_2001} and improved firm-worker matching found in \citet{Baum-Snow_Pavan_2012}.} 

Our estimates for the urban wage premium will be biased if there is sorting into cities by characteristics that affect wages but are distinct from age, race, and education. An example could be that workers sort into cities based on the industry they work in and differences in industrial wages can explain some of the remaining wage premium. \citet{Combes_Duranton_Gobillon_2008} show using panel data that individual fixed effects remove a sizeable portion of the variation in urban wage premium. Our time-series data is cross-sectional, so we can not use individual level fixed effects in our estimation. For our estimates to be causal, the exclusion restriction requires there to be no sorting beyond age, race, and education levels. 

The results from the regressions that do not include a measure of market access is found in Figure \ref{fig:premium_over_time} as the black lines with a diamond and a triangle marker. All regression tables can be found in Table \ref{table:ma_reg_all}. These lines show that the urban wage premium still decreases from a premium of around 30\% in 1940 through 1980. However, instead of the increase in the modern economy, our regression-based results show that the urban wage premium roughly levels off after 1980 at 20\% without controls and at around 16\% when controlling for age, education, and race. This result suggests that increased sorting of high-earning individuals into cities can explain the recent increase in urban wage premium found in the raw-measures. 

The final specification includes market access in the regression of the log of weekly wage on an urban indicator variable. This specification controls for the benefits cities receive from being located in an advantageous location in the transportation network. If part of the wage premium is explained by better location in the transportation network, we expect the premium to decrease when including market access which summarizes the network. The estimates on the urban indicator are found in Figure \ref{fig:premium_over_time} as the black line with circle markers and in Table \ref{table:ma_reg}. 

When controlling for the transportation advantage of cities and demographic differences, we see the urban indicator in 1940 has decreased to about 15\% from the 30\% observed in specifications that don't include market access. There still exists, albeit a much smaller, decline in urban wage premium through the 80s which flattens out to an urban wage premium that floats around 10\% until at least 2015. 

This result suggests that between 25.9\% to 47.7\% of the urban wage premium is explained by a relatively strong position in the transportation network.\footnote{The \% change in the urban wage indicator between the specification including controls and the specification inlcuding controls and market access is 47.7\% for 1940, 30.7\% for 1950, 35.9\% for 1960, 46.2\% for 1970, 35.4\% for 1980, 37.4\% for 1990, 26.1\% for 2000, 25.8\% for 2005, 33.1\% for 2010, and 27.4\% for 2015.} In particular, it appears that early in our sample, the differences in access to transportation network was large between urban and nonurban areas. This matches the fact that historical highway networks focused on connecting large cities and starting around 1955 began connecting many more places which increased market access for less populous areas (as evidenced by Figure \ref{fig:ma_dev_over_time}).

\section{MSA-level Market Access}\label{appendix:msa_ma}

We aggregate our county-level measure of Market Access from Equation \ref{eq:ma} to a measure at the urban area. For each year, we start with a crosswalk provided by IPUMS that lists which counties fall (at least partially) inside the boundaries of the metro area. Our metro area boundaries are the 1950 Standard Metropolitan Area for 1940 and 1950, the Metrpolitan Statitistical Areas for their respective year in 1960-2010, and the Core-Based Statistical Area for 2015. 

This list is modified slightly to include MSAs that are recorded in the IPUMS survey data but are not included in that year's crosswalk. The counties that the MSA covers are identified by hand using the shape files from NHGIS for that year or if missing from the previous year. 

For a given year, a metro area's market access is the geometric average of the corresponding counties' market access. Then, for our measure of market access for the nonurban portion of a state is the geometric average of all counties that are not within any metro areas. We include robustness checks for weighting the average by county population.
